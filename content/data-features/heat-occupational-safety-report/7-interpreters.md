We see another signal of this in workers who indicated that they would need an interpreter at a workers’ compensation hearing, an indicator that English may not be their primary language. On a 95°F day, their injury and illness risk increased by 15%, nearly twice the 8% increase among workers who did not indicate a need for an interpreter (Figure 7).

This pattern is consistent with broader research showing that immigrant workers and workers with limited English proficiency can face greater workplace safety risks. Language barriers can limit access to safety information and training, make it harder to communicate about hazards, and create barriers to speaking up about unsafe conditions.
