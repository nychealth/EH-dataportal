## Conclusion

This analysis points to how heat is dangerous for NYC workers across a range of job sectors and summer temperatures. Most heat-related injuries occur before temperatures become extremely hot, and the risks extend well beyond traditionally heat-exposed jobs and heat-related illness. The findings also point to broader social and neighborhood conditions that may shape workers’ vulnerability to heat. Together, these findings point to several considerations for workplace heat policy and prevention, summarized below.

1. Since most workplace injuries take place below heat advisory levels, enforceable protections for workers exposed to outdoor and indoor heat are needed **across a range of summer temperatures,** beginning at about 80F, with more protections on the hottest days.

2. Many types of workers, including those **working outdoors and indoors** in hot conditions, need workplace protections and trainings about how to stay safe in the heat. Workers and employers need a broader understanding of who is at risk from heat at work.

3. Improving awareness that **heat can cause more health problems than heat illness** is a step in preventing workplace injuries and heat-related worsening of underlying health conditions. Workers and employers should be trained to recognize early signs of heat exposure and how heat can impact health.

4. Guidance and outreach materials in **NYC’s many languages** and **partnering with immigrant community groups** on outreach and education is needed to reach workers most at risk.

5. Policies should recognize that workers may face **overlapping heat-related risks at work and at home** and account for cumulative vulnerability, including workplace and residential heat exposures, access to air conditioning, energy insecurity, building conditions, and access to health care.

6. **To account for workers not represented in this data,** reach workers through targeted outreach and alternative sources of information, including partnerships with workers and community organizations. Qualitative research with workers, employers and community organizations can also help better describe these work environments and suggest strategies to improve workplace safety.

7. **Protecting workers in informal or other work arrangements** is critical and will require pursuing tailored policies and practices.

The Health Department is working with other City agencies on heat safety guidance for employers and workers, as tasked by EO 17. EO 17 also requires every mayoral agency to develop and implement heat illness prevention plans for City employees and contractors, and it reinforces existing protections for outdoor workers, including access to bathrooms and workplace reporting requirements. To further support the City’s actions, the Health Department will also conduct interviews to understand employer perspectives and conditions for workers not captured in these data. Visit Nyc.gov/health/workplaceheat for heat safety guidance and resources for employers and workers.
