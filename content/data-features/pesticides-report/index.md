Local Law 37 was enacted on May 9, 2005, making New York City (NYC) the largest municipality in the nation to regulate agency use of pesticides on City-owned and -leased property. Local Law 54 of 2007 required agencies to report their pesticide use to the NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene (the Health Department) annually. This report summarizes pesticides applied by or on behalf of agencies on City-owned or leased property. When compared with total commercial pesticide use citywide, NYC agency use accounted for an estimated 2% of total volume of liquid pesticides and 20% of total pounds of solid pesticides (based on 2022 reference data, which is the most recent year for which New York State data have been processed). These estimates exclude non-toxic carbon dioxide rodenticide gas applications and mosquito larvicide applications, respectively.


Key findings:
- Pesticides were applied 842,666 times by NYC agencies in <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span>. Agencies used 27,230 gallons and 203,325 pounds of products (Table 1). The number of applications remained steady, but there was an increase in pesticide use by volume and weight from 2024. The increase in volume was due to higher levels of CO<sub>2</sub> gas rodenticide (a “best management practice” - pesticides with little potential for human exposure). There was an increase in total weight due to the increased use of pre-emergent solid herbicide to control weeds on golf courses.
- The Health Department conducts larviciding to prevent spread of West Nile virus and other mosquito-borne diseases. Larvicide applications in catch basins made mosquitoes the most targeted pest again in <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span>, followed by cockroaches and rats (Table 3).
- Fungicides represent the largest percentage of liquid pesticide products (57% by volume) used by City agencies, excluding CO<sub>2</sub> gas; insecticides represent the largest percentage of solid products used (36% by weight) (Table 1). Larvicides for mosquito control are charted separately from other solid insecticides, because they account for a large share of total insecticide product (78% by weight), and their year-to-year use varies more with weather than other insecticides. After excluding larvicides for mosquitoes, herbicides made up the largest percentage of solid products (43% by weight).
- Insecticides
  - Insecticides were the most frequently applied type of pesticide, accounting for 85% of all applications by count (Table 1).
  - Liquid insecticide use by volume was almost identical to 2024 (Figure 2).
  - Liquid pyrethroid products targeting cockroaches and bed bugs remained at historically low levels (Figure 6).
  - The number of mosquito larvicide applications and use of these products by weight remained steady compared with 2024.
  - “Best Management Practices” (BMP) insecticide products (Appendix 3) include gel and bait insecticides with little potential for human exposure. Use reported by weight was 73% lower in <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span> compared to 2024 and made up 50% of solid insecticide applications by weight after excluding larvicides. Applications to treat cockroaches declined 60% by weight from 2024 to <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span> after excluding larvicide. This apparent decline was relative to a 2024 spike that is most likely an artifact of how insect growth regulator (IGR) bait stations were reported (Figure 4).
- Herbicides
  - Liquid herbicide product use increased 19% from 2024 to <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span> (1,318 to 1,563 gallons) (Figure 2). Although there was a slight increase in <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span> (an increase of 90 gallons mostly on golf courses, professional sports stadiums, and roadways in and around parks), glyphosate use has declined 93% since 2012 (Figure 3). The NYC Parks Department has phased out use of glyphosate products in areas of parks accessible to the public as required by 2021 amendments to Local Law 37.
  - The use of solid herbicide products in <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span> more than doubled from 2024, primarily due to use of outdoor granular pre-emergent turf formulations on golf courses.
  - The Parks Department has mostly eliminated the use of synthetic herbicides, except for treatment of dangerous or invasive plants, as required by 2021 amendments to Local Law 37.
- Rodenticides
  - Use of solid rodenticide products decreased 17% by weight from 2024 (Figure 1). The Health Department calibrates rodenticide applications based on findings from neighborhood rat ‘indexing.’ There was a corresponding increase in use of CO<sub>2</sub> gas rodenticide (a “best management practice”).
 
## Pesticide use by NYC Agencies in <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span>

### Background

Local Law 37 was enacted on May 9, 2005. It established new requirements for pest control by NYC agencies, including the prohibition of certain products, posting warning notices prior to application, and annual pesticide use reporting. Prohibitions were phased in. By November 2005, EPA Toxicity Category 1 (signal word “danger”) was prohibited. By May 2006, use of pesticides classified by the EPA as of July 19, 2004, as possible, probable, or known human carcinogens was prohibited. Finally, by November 2006, the law prohibited use of pesticides classified as of March 4, 2005 by the State of California’s Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment as developmental toxicants.

Some exemptions were put in place, including use of products that posed little or no risk of human exposure, such as rodenticides in tamper-proof containers or applied directly into rat burrows or inaccessible areas, containerized insect bait, and non-toxic insecticides, such as boric acid, silica and diatomaceous earth. The law also exempted use of products to treat golf courses and professional sports fields, drinking and recreational water, and for other antimicrobial purposes. It empowered the NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene (the Health Department) to consider and grant requests for waivers from prohibition on a case-by-case basis. The Health Department issues citywide waivers annually for insecticide gels containing the ingredients fipronil, hydramethylnon, and avermectin due to their effectiveness and low potential for human exposure. All waivers and their original applications are available online.

In May 2021, Local Law 37 was amended to include prohibition of chemicals added to the EPA and California lists since the original law was passed. The amendment further prohibited use of any chemicals deemed probably or possibly carcinogenic by the World Health Organization’s International Association for Research on Cancer (IARC) as of May 2021, which included glyphosate, a commonly used herbicide listed as “probably carcinogenic to humans”. Additional prohibitions on use of synthetic pesticide chemicals in City parks went into effect in late 2022. Further exemptions were added for use of synthetic chemicals to control harmful or invasive species and in situations with worker safety concerns related to vehicular traffic. The impact of the prohibitions in City parks is evident in this report.

Local Law 54 of 2007 required New York City (NYC) Agencies to report their pesticide use to the Health Department annually. This report summarizes pesticides applied by or on behalf of agencies on City-owned or leased property in the preceding year.

### Sources of pesticide use data

Source data for each pesticide application includes the product name, EPA registration number, the quantity used (by weight or volume), the method of application, the target pest, the application date and location, and the agency or contractor responsible for the application. The Health Department uses the EPA’s pesticide information system to link each product – by registration number – to its pesticide type, class, toxicity category and active ingredients. In addition, the Health Department links products to the EPA Office of Pesticide Programs’ List of Chemicals Evaluated for Carcinogenic Potential, the State of California’s List of Chemicals Known to the State to Cause Cancer or Reproductive Toxicity, and the IARC carcinogen list, all available for download at LL37 Website.

### Methods

The Health Department hosts an electronic reporting tool, the New York City Pesticide Use Reporting System (NYCPURS), that all agencies, contractors, and licensed pest control applicators can register to use. Data from NYCPURS and some external electronic sources were imported into a single database, where they were checked for quality and validity. Weight and volumetric units were standardized to pounds and gallons, respectively. Products sold in solid form are primarily reported by weight and liquids and gases primarily by volume. Each record of data was assigned an agency identifier corresponding to the source agency. Data were analyzed using Microsoft SQL, Excel, and Access. We analyzed data by pesticide type and chemical class, and report total numbers, pounds, and gallons of applications for New York City and by agency on whose behalf the pesticides were applied.

#### Limitations in the data

- It is not possible to verify the completeness of agency reporting. Many agencies depend on contractors to maintain records and report applications to the Health Department.
- Staff and contractor turnover at agencies can lead to difficulty maintaining dependable and timely reporting.
- Year-to-year variation in pesticide use can be attributable to changes in practice, but it is also affected by budgets and the incidence of actual pest problems.
- Findings can be affected by decisions to report application counts by entire buildings vs. individual apartments or office suites.
- Findings can be affected by decisions to report quantities of pesticide used by diluted vs. undiluted amounts.
- NYCPURS reduces errors by restricting inputs to predefined values for some fields. However, if a liquid product is occasionally reported in pounds, it is included in the weight measure. More information about NYCPURS is available at LL37 Website.

### Types of pesticides

Four main types of pesticides were applied by agencies: rodenticides (products used to kill rodents); insecticides (to kill insects); herbicides (to kill plants); and fungicides (to kill molds and other fungi). 

#### Rodenticides
Most rodenticides applied by NYC agencies are second-generation anticoagulant rodenticides (SGARs), for example, brodifacoum, bromadiolone and difethialone. First-generation anticoagulants – such as warfarin, chlorophacinone and diphacinone – are rarely used. One advantage of SGARs is that they do not require multiple doses to kill a rat. However, there is growing awareness that SGAR use is contributing to widespread ecological harm, particularly among predatory birds and mammals, as they feed on poisoned rodents.

As an alternative to SGARs, some City agencies are using non-anticoagulant dry ice (frozen carbon dioxide) or CO<sub>2</sub> gas cannisters in sensitive outdoor locations, such as near City parks, building exteriors, squares and outdoor areas on school properties. In 2017, the Health Department worked with federal and state regulators to receive approval for this rodent control method, which has no risk of secondary poisoning to predatory animals (a “best management practice”).

#### Insecticides
Insecticides used by NYC agencies fall largely into three classes of products: pyrethrins and pyrethroids, organophosphates and carbamates, and “Best Management Practices” (BMP) products, which either use active ingredients with very low toxicity or involve formulation or application methods that minimize potential exposure and environmental spread. Biological insecticides are a type of BMP used to target mosquito larvae. They typically account for most agency insecticide applications by weight.

##### *Pyrethrins, Pyrethroids, and Synergists*
Pyrethrins and pyrethroids are less acutely toxic than the organophosphate and carbamate insecticides they have largely replaced. Accidental poisoning from professional applications is rare, though personal use of aerosols and foggers have been associated with acute exposures, poisonings and fires. Some pyrethroids may be respiratory allergens for sensitized people, including asthmatics. There is also concern over possible neurotoxicity from chronic exposure. EPA has classified pyrethrins, their synergists, and several pyrethroids – such as bifenthrin and cypermethrin – as suspected human carcinogens. Common pyrethroid active ingredients used by NYC agencies are cyfluthrin, cyhalothrin, esfenvalerate, and deltamethrin. Pyrethrins and pyrethroids break down relatively quickly when used outdoors but persist longer when applied indoors.

##### *Organophosphates and Carbamates*
Organophosphate and carbamate insecticides function by inhibiting the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, allowing the neurotransmitter acetylcholine to build up in tissues. Until EPA banned their use for most indoor applications in 1999 and 2000, organophosphates were the most heavily used class of insecticides. These products now represent a very small percentage of the insecticides used by City agencies, mostly driven by use on golf courses, which are exempt from Local Law 37 prohibitions.

##### *“Best Management Practices” Insecticides*
The market offers several highly effective insecticide products with little or no potential risk for human exposure. These products include biological pesticides for mosquito control and gels and baits for cockroach and other insect control; they are referred to as “Best Management Practices” insecticides (BMP). BMPs should be used as part of an integrated pest management approach. Integrated pest management involves removing garbage, fixing leaks and sealing cracks, to deprive pests of food, water and shelter; the aim is to minimize pesticide use.

Common active ingredients in BMPs include boric acid, silica gel, diatomaceous earth, the bacteria Bacillus sphaericus and Bacillus thuringiensis, hydramethylnon, fipronil, avermectin, and indoxacarb in containerized or gel form are also BMPs.

#### Herbicides
NYC agencies use herbicides primarily for vegetation control in parks, and for the maintenance of golf courses and roadsides, which are exempt when vehicular traffic puts maintenance workers at risk. Some prohibited products are used in parks and botanical gardens with waivers. The active ingredients most frequently used are triclopyr, butoxyethyl ester, imazamox and glufosinate-ammonium. These do not fall into the more toxic conventional herbicide classes that are common in the marketplace and described below. There is very little use by NYC agencies of conventional herbicides described below.

##### *Glyphosate*
Though use by NYC agencies has declined steeply, glyphosate remains a commonly used liquid herbicide in the US, best known as Roundup® products. Glyphosate is a broad-spectrum, non-selective systemic herbicide that can be used throughout the growing season. In early 2015, IARC changed the classification for glyphosate to “probable human carcinogen” (in conflict with a risk assessment by the European Food Safety Authority that determined glyphosate is “unlikely to pose a carcinogenic hazard to humans.”) Glyphosate is not acutely toxic, but there is concern that chronic exposure may increase risk of certain cancers. Glyphosate use had increased as agencies phased out the use of other herbicides prohibited under Local Law 37 but has been declining since 2013. The amendments to Local Law 37 passed in 2021 now prohibit the use of glyphosate products due to its IARC status as a probable carcinogen. Today, any use of glyphosate is most likely to occur on golf courses that are exempt from the product prohibition, or around roadsides where vehicle traffic creates a hazard to maintenance workers.

##### *Dinitroaniline*
Dinitroaniline-based herbicides, which are often combined with fertilizers, are used early in the growing season as pre-emergents. Several dinitroanilines are classified by EPA as possible human carcinogens, such as pendimethalin, trifluralin, and prodiamine. Dinitroaniline herbicides break down slowly.

##### *Chlorophenoxyacetic/Benzoic Acids*
The chlorophenoxyacetic acid herbicides are a family of related chemicals that are often combined in product formulations. Common active ingredients are 2,4-D, MCPP, dicamba, MCPA, and mecoprop-p. EPA classifies mecoprop-p as a possible carcinogen, and 2,4-D is considered by IARC as possibly carcinogenic to humans.

##### *Ureas*
Some urea herbicides are suspected carcinogens, and many are highly persistent in the environment. Common active ingredients are sulfometuron methyl, metsulfuron-methyl and halosulfuron-methyl.

#### Fungicides
Many fungicides are classified by EPA as suspected carcinogens. There is potential for exposure throughout the growing season because fungicides tend to be persistent, with half-lives measured in months and years. Degradation does not necessarily reduce the hazard, because breakdown products can also be toxic. The prohibited chemicals most frequently reported by NYC agencies under exemptions or waivers are chlorothalonil, tebuconazole, iprodione and thiophanate-methyl. Less toxic fungicides are also used, including azoxystrobin, fosetyl-Al, and trichoderma harzianum rifai strain KRL-AG2.

#### NYC agency pesticide use findings
Pesticide use can be measured and compared using frequency of application or quantity applied. Quantities for liquid applications are typically measured as volume in gallons and solid applications as weight in pounds. Patterns of use may look different depending on the measurement approach. Table 1 summarizes City agency pesticide use by pesticide type.

Pesticides were applied 842,666 times by NYC agencies in <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span>, totaling 27,230 gallons and 203,325 pounds of products. The pesticides applied most frequently by NYC agencies (by application count) were insecticides. Fungicides were the most heavily used products reported by volume, excluding CO<sub>2</sub> gas (57% by volume). Insecticides were the most heavily used solid products (36% by weight). In <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span>, 78% of the insecticides reported by weight were bacterial BMP products used by the Health Department mosquito-borne disease control programs. These products kill mosquito larvae in stagnant waters away from human contact. After excluding larvicides, herbicides were the most heavily used solid products (43% by weight), and 37% of solid pesticides by weight were rodenticides. Most rodenticide applied by City agencies is placed directly into rat burrows and poses little risk of exposure to the public.

##### Rodents
Rodenticide use decreased 17% by weight in <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span> (Figure 1), and the number of applications also decreased by 14%. Use varies year-to-year according to findings from the ongoing neighborhood rat surveillance and control initiative (Rat Indexing - NYC Health). The decrease in rodenticide by weight was due to decreased use of anticoagulant products. This decline was accompanied by an increased use of carbon dioxide (CO<sub>2</sub>) gas product (“best management practice”) in <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span>, making up almost all the 47% increase in rodenticide reported by volume.

##### Mosquitoes
Mosquito control measures include larviciding (killing larvae) and adulticiding (killing flying adults) – with larvicides applied primarily in catch basins and natural areas with standing water. The Health Department Vector Control program and their contractors were the primary applicators; 57,336 pounds of mosquito larvicide and 459 gallons of adulticide were applied in <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span>. The application count and amount of larvicide by weight remained steady compared with 2024 (Figure 1). The agency monitors mosquito populations across the five boroughs, especially the populations that carry pathogens such as the West Nile and Zika viruses. The amount of adulticide applied by the Health Department varies year to year depending on weather and severity of mosquito infestations. There was no significant change in the number of adulticide applications from 2024 to <span class="reportingYearPrint"></span> (data not shown).
